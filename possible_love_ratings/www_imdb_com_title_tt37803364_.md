[**Desire Looks to the Side Too**](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt37803364/reviews/?featured=rw11863903&ref_=tt_ururv_c_1_hd)

Lee Chang-dong has a frightening way of observing people until what first seemed like a perfectly understandable relationship begins to develop small cracks.

No one here desires only another person. Sometimes it's someone else's life. Their home. The security of being able to fail without everything collapsing. The feeling of waking up without having to calculate how much it costs to keep existing that way. Money moves through these relationships even when nobody is talking about it, and maybe that's what unsettled me the most. You can feel the distance between these people before the story ever needs to turn it into conflict.

Jeon Do-yeon is wonderful because Mi-ok seems to be constantly noticing things she would rather not notice. There's curiosity in her, but also a kind of painful fascination with being close to a life that could have been completely different. I really like how Lee allows contradictory feelings to remain on her face without trying to organize them. Sometimes I can't tell whether she's admiring someone, desiring what she sees, or beginning to resent the distance between them.

Maybe she doesn't know either.

And that makes every attempt at closeness feel a little dangerous.

The film finds so much discomfort in small things. A meal, someone offering money casually, a question asked with the best intentions and received almost as humiliation. There's no need for a big sign pointing toward where the violence is because it already exists in the difference between someone who can treat a certain amount of money as a detail and someone who has to reorganize their entire life around it.

Sul Kyung-gu carries that weight in a very physical way. Ho-seok seems to shrink as the world keeps reminding him that he is no longer needed. There's resentment there, but what moves me more is the shame. The feeling of someone who lost part of his identity along with his job and now has to keep existing in front of people who can't really imagine what that loss means.

Lee films all of this with a calm that sometimes feels almost cruel. The frame doesn't need to search for pain because it knows it will eventually appear on its own. The houses, restaurants, and workplaces seem to simply exist, but the difference between them slowly creates a very clear social geography. Who can occupy a certain space without thinking about it, and who enters already conscious of their own body.

And people keep trying to get closer.

That's what makes it all so sad to me.

Because there is genuine affection. There is real curiosity. But good intentions don't erase the distance between two lives, and the film understands how easy it is to turn an interest in someone else's suffering into another form of consumption. You want to understand that person, you want to record their story, maybe you even want to help them, but you can still go home afterward.

They still have to live there.

The length worked on me precisely because Lee lets these relationships change almost without announcement. By the time I noticed, a look that seemed innocent at the beginning was already carrying envy, guilt, or some desire no one had the courage to name. The film keeps accumulating these tiny shifts until you can no longer separate affection from need.

And maybe love is possible precisely inside that confusion.

Not a clean kind of love. A love contaminated by what we desire in other people, by the things we are ashamed to want, by the difference between being able to choose a life and simply trying to survive your own.

I left thinking that coveting may be less about wanting what someone has and more about looking at a person and imagining what it would feel like to exist with a little less weight.

Lee Chang-dong finds an enormous sadness in that thought. Because some people can desire someone else's life.

Others just want their own to stop hurting.

"Thou shalt not covet thy neighbor's goods."

Lee Chang-dong's Possible Love offers zero comfort. It uses the Tenth Commandment to strip away any idealized, romantic notions of class struggle. The story focuses on Mi-ok and Ho-seok, a couple left financially and emotionally hollowed out by a brutal factory strike, whose lives end up colliding with an elite documentary filmmaker, Ye-ji, and her wealthy husband, Sang-woo.

The film becomes deeply unsettling through its depiction of empathy. Ye-ji weaponizes Ho-seok's trauma for her project, turning his psychological collapse into content. At the same time, poverty drives Mi-ok into a quiet, desperate attraction toward Sang-woo. By their shared seaside trip, the class divide feels absolute. Lee strips away any hope for solidarity, proving that across this kind of wealth gap, every human connection eventually rots into a transaction.

The cast keeps this bleak story grounded. Jeon Do-yeon plays Mi-ok with a quiet, hard-edged pride, avoiding any obvious or showy acting. Sul Kyung-gu matches that intensity by filling Ho-seok with a silent burnout, where his anger simmers through every heavy movement.

Possible Love is a tough, bleak watch, but it stays with you. Lee Chang-dong shows, yet again, that nobody understands human desperation quite like he does. 🎬
